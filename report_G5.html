Introduction

Neurocutaneous syndrome, characterized by cerebral dysgenesis, neuropathy, ichthyosis, and keratoderma, or CEDNIK syndrome for short, is a autosomal recessive genetic disorder1. This disease is caused by a loss-of-function variant in SNAP29 (synaptosomal-associated protein 29), which encodes a SNARE protein, a vesicular membrane fusion protein2. Symptoms of the disease Microcephaly, Global developmental delay, Ataxia, Ichthyosis, Diffuse palmoplantar hyperkeratosis.

This syndrome was first discovered in 2005— within the 22q11.2 region and the SNAP29 gene —using homozygosity mapping on two unrelated families with a history of consanguineous marriage that exhibited similar symptoms3. In the subsequent years, up to 2021, nearly 20 variants of this syndrome were discovered4.

CEDNIK syndrome is an extremely rare disorder, with an estimated prevalence of less than 1 per 1,000,000 individuals; a reliable population-based incidence has not been established due to the small number of reported cases5.

Variant detection and diagnosis have become much more accessible with the advent of next-generation sequencing (NGS). Unlike earlier sequencing technologies, NGS enables the simultaneous analysis of anything from a few genes to hundreds of genes, the entire exome, or even the whole genome. NGS systems employ approaches such as whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and whole-exome sequencing (WES)6.

In this report, we focus on WES because Sydnik syndrome is a Mendelian monogenic disorder; identifying variations within the exons alone is generally sufficient for its detection. In the case we analyzed, a 3-year-old girl exhibited symptoms of the syndrome while her parents were healthy; therefore, our objective was to perform WES for this family of three and identify the variant by comparing the results against the reference human genome (GRCh37/hg19). This type of analytical method is known as “trio analysis”7, and given that this syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder, we can infer that both parents are heterozygous carriers1.

Study Participants/Whole exome sequencing

The base of our work is publicly available genetic data from a Spanish family, here consisting of a father, mother and their daughter, referred to as “the Corpasome”8. In our case the genome of the daughter is synthetically altered and a disease phenotype called CEDNIK Syndrome, caused by these alterations, is diagnosed. Main symptoms are developmental delay and skin disorder. The latter mainfasting at the age of two in ichthyosis and thickening of palms and soles. Further, she has been diagnosed with microcephaly and poor motor development as well as impaired coordination. As Cednik Syndrome is an autosomal recessive inheritable disease and the parents are healthy, the father and mother both have one chromosome with the disease-causing variant and one without. After deciding to make their genomes publicly available, the Corpas family bought direct-to-consumer sequencing privately to collect DNA in saliva using Oragene kits. Exomes were captured with Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon (44 Mb design) and sequenced on Illumina HiSeq with an average coverage of about 30x over the target region.

NGS data preprocessing

Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) data pre-processing was used to refine and align the raw reads given to downstream Whole-Exome Sequencing (WES) analysis. The input data set comprised individual raw FASTQ (.fq) files corresponding to the family trio: mother, father, and affected child, from the Corpasome family. They are organized for each person as as paired-ends reads, 150 bp per read for R1 and R2.

Using the raw data (.fq) as an input, we can graph and see the organization of the sequences, GC percentage and the quality scores through the FastQC Report (version 0.12.1) in .fqc files. We considerate the Q scores above 30 (Q30), in acordance to the standart Illumina quality trimming sequence9.

To process the alignments, the “Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (bwa mem)”10 tool converts our .fq files into .bam/.sam files by comparing the qualities and positions against a reference genome; which, for this study was hg19. With the binary version (.bam), we can easly access to specific genomic regions using index files (.bai), allowing the reads of each family member to be processed and indexed efficiently in the vcf files.

A selection of variants is uptake from the comparison form specific regions identified on the .bai files from the comparison of the hg19 reference genome. To refine the search for possible SNPs, we cross-reference with the dbSNP database for hg19, allowing the algorithm to prevent confusing a real mutation with a sequencing error when re calibrating base qualities.

Using the refined regions of interest, we call GATK3 (ver 4.1.4.1) to perform variant calling on the BAM files using the “HaplotypeCaller” function. This tool performs a local de novo assembly of haplotypes in active regions, allowing it to detect both SNPs and complex indels with high precision11. Finally, the resulting variants are stored as VCF files that could be converted into BCF.

Finally, to process the detected genetic variants, the ANNOVAR tool helped merge the resulting variant compilation into a readable table. This table in .tsv format contains genetic annotations, known identifiers, predictive scores, clinical relevance, and genomic coordinates12.

Variant calling/filtering

To begin the variant calling, the raw file includes all covered positions in exons which could potentially contain variants. It includes all three samples and displays 1,418,395 positions. After the variant calling 8,803 of these positions are defined as SNPs in the bcf file which is about 0.62% of the positions in the raw file. To refine the variant calling, positions with a read depth below 20 are removed. 5,898 positions remain in the vcf file. These are 5,983 variants if multi-allelic sites are considered and split in separate rows. This process is also called demultiplexing. Further conversion to tsv and annotation using ANNOVAR, does not change the number of variants. The resulting file with 5,983 variants can now be used for downstream analysis and filtering of possible disease-causing variants.

Besides ensuring sufficient read depth, the transition-transversion ratio as well as the nonsynonymous/synonymous SNP ratio are quality metrics to control for correct variant calling Since transitions require less energy, the ratio of transition-transversion is expected to be > 2 which is also reflected in the data presenting a ratio of 2.44. Regarding the nonsynonymous/synonymous SNP ratio a 1 to 1.2 ratio is usual. Our data presents a ratio of 0.99.

To identify the disease-causing variant, a comparison with the parental genomes is useful to find a possible recessive pattern. Out of the 5,983 variants, 153 were found to be recessive. Additionally, a filter is applied to discard common variants which in this case in only one variant. Since exonic variants are the most probable kind of variant to alter the gene product namely the protein, they are of biggest interest in this case report. The next filtering step removes all nonexonic variants and further filtering is applied to remove synonymous variants as well as nonframeshift deletions or insertions because their effect on the protein is usually not pathogenic. Finally, 23 variants in 11 different genes are left.

In order to select the disease-causing gene out of these 11 candidate genes, the varsome database is used to evalute related phenotypes. Some of these genes are not associated with a disease phenotype (CCT8L2, ZNF280A) or related with different types of cancer (ATP6V1E1, MED15, RFPL2, XRCC6, NHP2L1, ATP5L2. BRD1, ZBED4). The SNAP29 gene, however, is related to the CEDNIK syndrome - an autosomal recessive inherited disease. Furthermore, related HPO terms, namely ichthyosis, palmoplantar keratoderma and neurophathy, match with the described symptoms of the patient.
