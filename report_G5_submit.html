Case report: CEDNIK SYNDROME

Introduction

Neurocutaneous syndrome, characterized by cerebral dysgenesis, neuropathy, ichthyosis, and keratoderma, or CEDNIK syndrome for short, is a autosomal recessive genetic disorder1. This disease is caused by a loss-of-function variant in SNAP29 (synaptosomal-associated protein 29), which encodes a SNARE protein, a vesicular membrane fusion protein2. Symptoms of the disease Microcephaly, Global developmental delay, Ataxia, Ichthyosis, Diffuse palmoplantar hyperkeratosis.

This syndrome was first discovered in 2005— within the 22q11.2 region and the SNAP29 gene —using homozygosity mapping on two unrelated families with a history of consanguineous marriage that exhibited similar symptoms3. In the subsequent years, up to 2021, nearly 20 variants of this syndrome were discovered4.

CEDNIK syndrome is an extremely rare disorder, with an estimated prevalence of less than 1 per 1,000,000 individuals; a reliable population-based incidence has not been established due to the small number of reported cases5.

Variant detection and diagnosis have become much more accessible with the advent of next-generation sequencing (NGS). Unlike earlier sequencing technologies, NGS enables the simultaneous analysis of anything from a few genes to hundreds of genes, the entire exome, or even the whole genome. NGS systems employ approaches such as whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and whole-exome sequencing (WES)6.

In this report, we focus on WES because Sydnik syndrome is a Mendelian monogenic disorder; identifying variations within the exons alone is generally sufficient for its detection. In the case we analyzed, a 3-year-old girl exhibited symptoms of the syndrome while her parents were healthy; therefore, our objective was to perform WES for this family of three and identify the variant by comparing the results against the reference human genome (GRCh37/hg19). This type of analytical method is known as “trio analysis”7, and given that this syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder, we can infer that both parents are heterozygous carriers1.

Study Participants/Whole exome sequencing

The base of our work is publicly available genetic data from a Spanish family, here consisting of a father, mother and their daughter, referred to as “the Corpasome”8. In our case the genome of the daughter is synthetically altered and a disease phenotype called CEDNIK Syndrome, caused by these alterations, is diagnosed. Main symptoms are developmental delay and skin disorder. The latter mainfasting at the age of two in ichthyosis and thickening of palms and soles. Further, she has been diagnosed with microcephaly and poor motor development as well as impaired coordination. As Cednik Syndrome is an autosomal recessive inheritable disease and the parents are healthy, the father and mother both have one chromosome with the disease-causing variant and one without. After deciding to make their genomes publicly available, the Corpas family bought direct-to-consumer sequencing privately to collect DNA in saliva using Oragene kits. Exomes were captured with Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon (44 Mb design) and sequenced on Illumina HiSeq with an average coverage of about 30x over the target region.

NGS data preprocessing

Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) data pre-processing was used to refine and align the raw reads given to downstream Whole-Exome Sequencing (WES) analysis. The input data set comprised individual raw FASTQ (.fq) files corresponding to the family trio: mother, father, and affected child, from the Corpasome family. They are organized for each person as as paired-ends reads, 150 bp per read for R1 and R2.

Using the raw data (.fq) as an input, we can graph and see the organization of the sequences, GC percentage and the quality scores through the FastQC Report (version 0.12.1) in .fqc files. We considerate the Q scores above 30 (Q30), in acordance to the standart Illumina quality trimming sequence9.

To process the alignments, the “Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (bwa mem)”10 tool converts our .fq files into .bam/.sam files by comparing the qualities and positions against a reference genome; which, for this study was hg19. With the binary version (.bam), we can easly access to specific genomic regions using index files (.bai), allowing the reads of each family member to be processed and indexed efficiently in the vcf files.

A selection of variants is uptake from the comparison form specific regions identified on the .bai files from the comparison of the hg19 reference genome. To refine the search for possible SNPs, we cross-reference with the dbSNP database for hg19, allowing the algorithm to prevent confusing a real mutation with a sequencing error when re calibrating base qualities.

Using the refined regions of interest, we call GATK3 (ver 4.1.4.1) to perform variant calling on the BAM files using the “HaplotypeCaller” function. This tool performs a local de novo assembly of haplotypes in active regions, allowing it to detect both SNPs and complex indels with high precision11. Finally, the resulting variants are stored as VCF files that could be converted into BCF.

Finally, to process the detected genetic variants, the ANNOVAR tool helped merge the resulting variant compilation into a readable table. This table in .tsv format contains genetic annotations, known identifiers, predictive scores, clinical relevance, and genomic coordinates12.

Variant calling/filtering

To begin the variant calling, the raw file includes all covered positions in exons which could potentially contain variants. It includes all three samples and displays 1,418,395 positions. After the variant calling 8,803 of these positions are defined as SNPs in the bcf file which is about 0.62% of the positions in the raw file. To refine the variant calling, positions with a read depth below 20 are removed. 5,898 positions remain in the vcf file. These are 5,983 variants if multi-allelic sites are considered and split in separate rows. This process is also called demultiplexing. Further conversion to tsv and annotation using ANNOVAR, does not change the number of variants. The resulting file with 5,983 variants can now be used for downstream analysis and filtering of possible disease-causing variants.

Since exonic variants are the most probable kind of variant to alter the gene product namely the protein, they are of biggest interest in this case report. In order to select the disease-causing gene out of these 11 candidate genes, the varsome database is used to evalute related phenotypes. Some of these genes are not associated with a disease phenotype (CCT8L2, ZNF280A) or related with different types of cancer (ATP6V1E1, MED15, RFPL2, XRCC6, NHP2L1, ATP5L2. BRD1, ZBED4). The SNAP29 gene, however, is related to the CEDNIK syndrome - an autosomal recessive inherited disease. Furthermore, related HPO terms, namely ichthyosis, palmoplantar keratoderma and neurophathy, match with the described symptoms of the patient.

Analysis of the candidate gene

The identified variant can be found at position 90 where arginine is exchanged by proline due to a transition from guanine to cytosine at position 21,224,656 in the chromosome 22. Using the UCSC liftover tool the variant at the position chr22:21224656 in the hg19 reference genome could be mapped to chr22:20870368 in the hg38 genome13. This is useful to search databases including both genomes to find also newly added findings relying on the hg38 genome. The nomenclature used for database search is chr22:g.21224656G>C on genomic level refering to hg19, c.269G>C on cDNA level and p.Arg90Pro for protein level.

At the variant position, the read depth is sufficient for a reliable genotype call in all three samples with 78 reads covering the position in the father, 72 in the mother and 73 in the daughter. Both parents are heterozygous and show a similar allele ratio of about 53 % guanine and 47 % cytosine. The daughter is homozygous for the alternative allele, with 90 % of her reads showing cytosine. This is consistent with a recessive mode of inheritance, because both parents are unaffected carriers and the affected child has two copies of the variant. Remarkably, about 10 % of the daughter’s reads show guanine. This is unlikely to reflect a true second allele, but rather shows a technical artifact, such as sequencing errors, misaligned reads or even a contamination with parental DNA.

At the considered position which is in the middle of the exon, the coverage is above 70x allowing further analysis based on the sequenced nucleotides. In our case it is important to mention that these high coverage values are caused by the synthetic introduction of the variant rather than showing real sample reads.

The variant 22-21224656-G-C has not been reported in the population database gnomAD which hints towards an extremely rare variant14. This finding fits well with the observed phenotype as the CEDNIK syndrome is a very rare disease. At this position, variants of G-A and G-T have been reported, but also with a very rare allele frequency which underlines the hypothesis further. In gnomAD, SNAP29 shows no depletion of missense variants (expected: 148.1, observed 178, o/e 1.20, Z = -0.87). For predicted loss-of-function variants, 12.2 SNVs would be expected and 5 have been observed14. This could point to weak depletion, but with so few observed variants the estimate is imprecise, and the data do not provide convincing evidence of constraint. This pattern is expected for a gene associated with an autosomal recessive disorder because heterozygous carriers are unaffected meaning that deleterious alleles are not strongly selected against and can persist with a low frequency in the population. In conclusion, these findings do not support nor argue against the candidate gene. Additionally, these metrics conclude on the whole gene and cannot distinguish between different variants. Consequently, further evaluation with data specific for the detected variant is necessary.

This can be done using varsome15. Again, the exact variant cannot be found, but predictions done by AlphaMissense and EVE show that this variant is potentially “pathogenic moderate”. Furthermore, the PhastCons100way conservation score of 1.000 and the PhyloP100way score of 4.218 underline that this nucleotide is highly conserved across different vertebras and thereby, probable to be pathogenic if altered.

This visualization from the UCSC Genome Browser13 shows how conserved this position and neighboring nucleotides and amino acids are across different species. The database further depicts the different tissues where SNAP29 is expressed. As the protein is related to fusion of cellular membranes for example during autophagy16, it is expressed in a variety of tissues. Beneath them, brain, skin and skeletal muscle which are affected in the case of the reported child. This enhances SNAP29 being the relevant cause for the observed symptoms. Besides that, other variants within SNAP29 have been reported to be causative for the CEDNIK syndrome16. Some examples are a missense variant exchanging glutamate to lysine at position 89, the depletion of leucine at position 119 or methione to valine at position 184. The variant R90P lies within the C-terminus of the Snare region which is anchored in the vesicle membrane17 1819. This region builds an alpha helix. Since the exchanged amino acid is an arginine which supports alpha helical structures to a proline which is known to be incompatible with helical structures due to its missing NH-structure due to its ring formation20. The protein folding is assumed to be disturbed. The framework of Franklin suggests a classification of VUS for the variant naming low frequencies in gnomAD population databases and in-silico predictions supporting deleterious effects21.

The protein structure derived using the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database22 shows the exact position of Arg90 in the alpha helix in the SNARE region. A predicted model of p.Arg90Pro does not show a break in the helix as expected. However, AlphaFold was not designed to predict effects of single substitutions and in the cell, the structure might still be destabilised or different from the prediction.

Discussion

In this study, Whole Exome Sequencing (WES) trio data from the Corpasome family were processed and analyzed to identify the genetic cause of a severe neurocutaneous disorder observed in a 3-year-old female patient. Following a bioinformatics workflow that included raw FASTQ quality control, alignment to the hg19 reference genome using BWA-MEM, duplicate removal, base quality recalibration, and variant calling with GATK3 HaplotypeCaller, a total of 5,983 variants were identified across all family members and annotated using ANNOVAR. Subsequent functional filtering and inheritance model-based prioritization steps successfully isolated a causative missense variant (p.Arg90Pro) in the SNAP29 gene, located at position 21,224,656 on chromosome 22 (G>C). The mode of inheritance for this variant and the associated CEDNIK syndrome is well-known to be autosomal recessive, which was confirmed by observing that both healthy parents are heterozygous carriers while the affected patient is homozygous for the variant.

To support the hypothesis that we have identified the true causative variant, several lines of converging evidence were established. First, sequential bioinformatic filtering—moving from 5,983 total variants down to 11 candidate genes and 23 variants—isolated the SNAP29 missense variant (p.Arg90Pro) based on an autosomal recessive inheritance model and rarity criteria. Second, the variant is completely absent from the gnomAD population database, confirming its extreme rarity in concordance with the prevalence of CEDNIK syndrome. Third, high-stringency quality metrics and coverage analysis demonstrated that the variant position is exceptionally well-covered (>70x read depth) across all family members, with the affected child being homozygous for the mutant allele and both parents acting as heterozygous carriers, as visualized clearly in IGV. Finally, cross-referencing with HPO terms and VarSome demonstrated that SNAP29 is uniquely linked to CEDNIK syndrome features (such as microcephaly, ichthyosis, and palmoplantar keratoderma), whereas all other candidate genes are either unassociated with disease or linked exclusively to cancer pathways.
